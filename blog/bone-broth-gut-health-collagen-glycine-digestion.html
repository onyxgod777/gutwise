Bone Broth for Gut Health: Collagen, Glycine, and the Real Evidence

September 13, 2026 · 9 min read · ← Blog

Few foods carry as much folklore as bone broth. It shows up in every traditional cuisine on earth — Chinese long-simmered stocks, Jewish chicken soup, French fonds, Japanese tonkotsu. Grandmothers prescribed it for colds, convalescence, and upset stomachs long before anyone had a word for collagen peptides or glycine. In the last decade it has been reborn as a wellness staple, sold in cafés as a sipping drink and credited with everything from smoother skin to a sealed gut lining.

So what is actually true? As with most things in the gut-health world, the honest answer is more interesting than either the hype or the dismissal. Bone broth is a genuinely useful, gentle, nutrient-dense food — and it is also not a miracle cure. Let's look at what it contains, what those compounds actually do, and how to use it sensibly as part of a broader approach to digestive health.

What Actually Happens When You Simmer Bones

When bones, connective tissue, and cartilage are simmered for hours in water — ideally with a splash of acid like vinegar — the heat and hydrolysis break long, tough protein chains into shorter, water-soluble fragments. What ends up in your mug is largely:

  • Gelatin and collagen peptides — hydrolysed collagen, rich in glycine, proline, and hydroxyproline
  • Minerals — calcium, magnesium, phosphorus, and potassium, though in amounts that are far more modest than most people assume
  • Glutamine and other amino acids — released from muscle and connective tissue
  • Glycosaminoglycans — including glucosamine and chondroitin, in small quantities
  • Electrolytes — which is why broth feels so restorative after illness or exertion

Notice what is not on that list: fiber. Broth feeds your microbiome only indirectly. Its value is as a nourishing, easily digested liquid — not as a prebiotic.

Collagen, Gelatin, and Your Intestinal Lining

The most repeated claim about bone broth is that it "heals leaky gut." That phrasing is too strong — no food seals a compromised intestinal barrier on its own. But there is a reasonable mechanistic story worth understanding.

Your intestinal lining is a single layer of cells held together by tight junctions. Between those cells sits connective tissue built largely from collagen. Gelatin, the cooked form of collagen, is unusually easy to digest and provides the raw amino acids your body uses to maintain that matrix. Some research suggests that specific collagen peptides may support the integrity of the gut barrier and help calm irritation in the lining — though most of this work is preliminary, and human trials are limited.

Where the evidence is clearer is in comfort. Broth is warm, salty, low-fiber, and low-residue. During a flare, after a stomach bug, or while recovering from a course of antibiotics, that makes it one of the easiest things your digestive system will accept. Glycine: The Quiet Amino Acid

Glycine is the smallest amino acid and one of the most abundant in the body, yet most modern diets are surprisingly low in it. Bone broth and gelatin are among the richest food sources available. Glycine matters for gut health in a few ways:

  • It is a building block of collagen — roughly one third of every collagen molecule is glycine
  • It supports glutathione production — your master antioxidant, heavily used in the gut and liver
  • It acts as an inhibitory neurotransmitter — which may partly explain the calm, sleepy feeling many people get from a cup of broth in the evening
  • It supports bile acid conjugation — a liver process that is essential to digesting fats properly

Glutamine, the Barrier, and the Gut's Fuel of Choice

The cells lining your small intestine — enterocytes — preferentially burn the amino acid glutamine as fuel. During stress, illness, or after injury, glutamine demand rises sharply. Broth contains glutamine, though in modest amounts; a serious therapeutic dose is usually delivered as a supplement rather than a bowl of soup. Still, for everyday nourishment, broth adds to the pool.

Your colon's lining has a different favorite fuel: butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid made when your gut bacteria ferment fiber. Broth cannot supply that. This is the key insight — broth nourishes you; fiber nourishes your microbes. You need both.

What Bone Broth Is Not

An honest assessment matters, because the wellness industry rarely offers one:

  • It is not a protein powerhouse. A cup typically delivers well under 10 grams of protein, and it is not a complete protein. It complements other protein sources; it does not replace them.
  • It is not a reliable calcium supplement. Mineral content varies wildly with the bones used and the simmering time. Don't count on broth for your calcium needs.
  • It is not a cure for leaky gut or autoimmune disease. Anyone promising that is overselling. Think of it as one supportive input among many.
  • It is not a weight-loss trick. A "broth fast" works mostly because you are eating and drinking very little. That is not a gut-healing strategy; it is a fast with marketing.

Who Should Be Careful With Broth

Two groups deserve a note. First, anyone with histamine intolerance — long-simmered stocks are naturally high in histamine, and the longer a pot simmers, the more it accumulates. If you are sensitive, a fresh, short-simmered broth (or none at all) may suit you better. Learn more about histamine intolerance and the DAO enzyme →

Second, anyone watching sodium. Broth is salty by nature, and commercial versions vary enormously — some cartons carry a third of a day's sodium in a single cup. If blood pressure is a concern, read labels or make your own without added salt.

Making It — or Choosing It Well

If you make broth at home, the process is forgiving: quality bones (roasted first for flavour), cold water, a tablespoon of vinegar, a low simmer for 8–24 hours, skim, strain, cool, and lift off the fat. It should gel in the fridge — a sign of good gelatin content. If it doesn't gel, you simply had fewer connective-tissue-rich parts in the pot; the flavour and minerals are still there.

If you buy it, look for a short ingredient list, no added flavourings or yeast extract, and a real protein content you can see on the label. Powdered collagen peptides are a legitimate and convenient alternative — they dissolve in anything and deliver a standardized dose, though they skip the minerals and electrolytes that make broth so pleasant.

Using Broth as Part of a Gut-Friendly Day

The most useful way to think about broth is as a ritual rather than a remedy. A warm savoury drink in the morning replaces the coffee-and-empty-stomach habit that many people find irritating. A mug in the late afternoon can head off the salt-and-sugar craving that often strikes when energy dips. A cup in the evening pairs naturally with winding down.

Build the rest of the day around the fundamentals that actually change your microbiome over months, not days:

  • Variety of plants — aim for 30 different plant foods a week to feed diverse bacteria
  • Fermented foods — a small daily serving of yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, or kimchi
  • Fiber from whole foods — the true fuel for butyrate production
  • Meal rhythm — eating in daylight hours, with real gaps between meals, so your gut can run its cleaning waves
  • Sleep and movement — the gut and the circadian system are deeply intertwined

💡 A grounded view. Bone broth is traditional, gentle, and genuinely nourishing — and it is not magic. The people who get the most from it are the ones who combine it with fiber, fermented foods, sleep, and consistency. No single food carries a whole gut.

There is something quietly sensible about the old practice of keeping a pot on the stove. It is slow, cheap, and made from parts most kitchens throw away. In a wellness world obsessed with the next supplement, broth is a reminder of a principle GutWise returns to again and again: the body responds to nourishment and rhythm, not to novelty or extremes.
